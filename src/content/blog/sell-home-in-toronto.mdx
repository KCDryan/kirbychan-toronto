## The short answer

The steps to sell home in Toronto run in roughly the same order for most owners: decide who will represent you, price the house, prepare what you must disclose, clear up your mortgage and anything registered on title, handle the offers and then let your lawyer close. The last stretch has a few City of Toronto steps that are easy to miss because they happen after the sign comes down.

This guide walks through each step in order and points to our more detailed posts where a step deserves its own page. It does not cover commission or tax in depth, because we have separate posts on [selling a house in Ontario commission](/blog/selling-a-house-in-ontario-commission/) and [selling a house in Ontario taxes](/blog/selling-a-house-in-ontario-taxes/). It is general information, not legal or tax advice. Your real estate lawyer and accountant should confirm anything that depends on your own situation.

## Sell home in Toronto: the steps at a glance

| Step | What you decide or do | Who helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Representation | Choose a registered agent or decide to sell yourself | RECO's Public Register, your lawyer |
| 2. Price | Set a list price from comparable sales | Your agent or an appraiser |
| 3. Disclosure | Know which defects you must tell buyers about | Your lawyer |
| 4. Title and mortgage | Check the prepayment penalty and any notices on title | Your lender, your lawyer |
| 5. Offers | Decide what, if anything, to share about competing offers | Your agent |
| 6. Deposit and conditions | Confirm where the deposit is held and when the deal is firm | Your agent, your lawyer |
| 7. Closing | Final meter read, payment plans, Vacant Home Tax | Your lawyer, the City |

If you are also buying, the order of the two deals is its own decision. Our post on [whether to sell first or buy first](/blog/sell-first-or-buy-first-downsizing-toronto/) sets out the trade-offs.

## Step 1: choose who will represent you

The Real Estate Council of Ontario (RECO) regulates every agent and brokerage in the province. Its advice to sellers is practical:

- **Check registration.** RECO's Public Register shows whether a salesperson is registered and in good standing, along with any disciplinary action.
- **Interview more than one.** RECO recommends at least three agents. Ask about their experience in your neighbourhood and the kinds of homes they have recently sold.
- **Ask how the home will be marketed.** RECO gives staging, professional photos, advertising and open houses as examples of services to ask about.
- **Ask for references** from past clients and follow up with them.
- **Read the agreement before you sign.** RECO says to review the contract carefully with the agent and to consult a lawyer if you are unsure.

What you will pay and what the listing agreement must say about it are covered in our [commission post](/blog/selling-a-house-in-ontario-commission/). Our [sellers' page](/sellers/) explains how we run a sale from price to closing.

### Selling without an agent

You can sell a Toronto home yourself, but RECO warns there are significant risks if you lack the knowledge and expertise. You become responsible for valuing the property, setting the price, handling competing offers and preparing all documents. The buyer's agent works for the buyer: anything you tell them may be passed on, including your lowest acceptable price and they cannot give you advice or opinions. Before helping you, they must give you RECO's Information and Disclosure to Self-represented Party form and explain it. RECO recommends independent professional advice before you proceed and notes that you can become a brokerage's client at any point in the transaction.

## Step 2: set the price

A list price should come from homes like yours, not from a headline average. An agent's comparable sales analysis and a formal appraisal are two different things and each has its place. Our post on [Toronto home appraisal](/blog/toronto-home-appraisal/) explains what an appraiser checks and when you might want one. For a planning number from recent comparable sales, start with our [free home valuation](/home-valuation/).

When you set the price, also work out what you expect to keep after the sale costs. Our breakdown of [Toronto downsizing costs](/blog/toronto-downsizing-costs/) lists the bills a seller usually faces, from commission and legal fees to moving.

## Step 3: know what you have to disclose

Buyers will ask about the house and some answers are not optional. RECO's guidance to agents sets out the two categories of defect that Canadian law recognizes:

- **Patent defects** are ones a buyer can detect on a reasonable inspection. A seller does not have to disclose them, though RECO cautions that a seller should never try to conceal one.
- **Latent defects** are ones that would make a property unfit for habitation, dangerous or potentially dangerous and that are generally not apparent on inspection. A seller must disclose latent defects.

RECO adds that a seller may still be liable for a latent defect they did not know about if they were wilfully blind to it. It also notes that these obligations come from case law, not from the Trust in Real Estate Services Act (TRESA). RECO says an agent should advise a seller to get a legal opinion where there is any concern. If something about your house worries you, raise it with your lawyer before the listing goes live, not after an offer arrives.

## Step 4: check your mortgage and your title

Two things registered against the house can change what you walk away with or delay the closing.

### Your mortgage penalty

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) says a prepayment penalty may apply if you pay back your entire mortgage before the end of the term, including when you sell. The penalty is usually the higher of 3 months' interest on what you still owe or the interest rate differential. FCAC tells borrowers to check with their lender for the actual amount. If you are buying another home, FCAC also suggests asking your lender about porting, which means taking your existing rate, terms and conditions to the new home. Ask early, because the answer can affect your closing date.

### Rented equipment and notices of security interest

If your house has a rented water heater, furnace or air conditioner, check how it shows up on title. In the past, some companies registered a Notice of Security Interest (NOSI) on title for that equipment. The Government of Ontario says that on June 6, 2024 the Homeowner Protection Act became law, prohibiting NOSIs for consumer goods and deeming all consumer NOSIs already registered to be expired. They can still show up in a title search but no longer affect the land.

Ontario says there is no need to act immediately, but if you want one removed during a sale, a lawyer can file the documents with the Land Registry. The province also warns that some companies contact homeowners offering to remove NOSIs for a fee. You do not have to pay to remove an expired consumer NOSI and Ontario says to report such offers to local police.

## Step 5: decide how you will handle competing offers

When more than one buyer makes an offer, RECO's rules give you the choice of how open the process is:

1. Buyers who have made an offer are entitled to know how many competing offers there are. You can also direct your agent to share the number with other interested buyers.
2. Your agent cannot share any of the content of the offers unless you direct them to. You do not have to share it.
3. If you do want details shared, you must give your agent clear written direction first.
4. Personal or identifying information in an offer can never be shared.
5. You can decide before or after offers arrive and change your direction at any time.

Talk this through with your agent before the listing starts, so the instructions are in writing when the first offer comes in.

## Step 6: the deposit and the conditions

Once you accept an offer, the buyer pays the deposit set out in it. Where a brokerage holds the deposit, RECO's consumer deposit insurance, in which every registered broker and salesperson must participate by law, covers it in events such as brokerage theft, fraud, insolvency or misappropriation of funds. Coverage is up to $200,000 per claim and up to $4 million for all claims from a single event, at no cost to you.

It does not cover a disagreement between buyer and seller. If a deal falls apart and the two sides cannot agree on who gets the deposit, RECO says it can only be released with a signed mutual release or a court order. Read any conditions in the offer, such as financing or an inspection, with your agent and lawyer, so you know the exact date the deal becomes firm.

If you are selling a condo, the buyer will usually want the status certificate. Our post on the [condo status certificate in Toronto](/blog/condo-status-certificate-toronto/) explains what it contains and how long the corporation has to provide it.

## Before you close: the City of Toronto steps

Your lawyer handles the transfer itself. A few City of Toronto items still need you or your lawyer to act.

### Property tax and your utility account

- **Ownership change.** The City updates the tax account when it receives a lawyer's letter with the previous and new owners' names, the address, the assessment roll number, the new owner's mailing address, the closing date and the lawyer's signature. The online address tool cannot change ownership.
- **Tax and Utility Certificate.** The City suggests consulting your lawyer about obtaining one, as it shows the current status of the tax and utility accounts.
- **Pre-authorized payments.** You must notify the City in writing to cancel the Pre-Authorized Tax Payment Program or the Pre-Authorized Utility Bill Payment Program. Enrolment does not move with you to another property.
- **Final water meter read.** To finalize the utility account, contact the City with the property address, utility account number, closing date, a forwarding address and a phone number. The final bill includes a Special/Final Read Fee and must be paid another way.
- **Adjustments.** The City points owners to the statement of adjustments in the sale documents or to their lawyer to see what is owed in property tax up to closing. Our [Toronto property tax](/blog/toronto-property-tax/) post explains how the bill is calculated.

### The Vacant Home Tax declaration

The City of Toronto's Vacant Home Tax is 3 per cent of the property's Current Value Assessment. Owners declare the occupancy status of their property for each tax year. The 2026 declaration is due by April 30, 2027 and a property with no declaration is treated as vacant.

For sellers, the timing of the sale decides who declares. If the closing falls within the tax year being declared, either the buyer or the seller can submit it and the property qualifies for the transfer of ownership exemption. That exemption does not cover adding or removing an owner or a name change. If you sell after the tax year ends, the City says the seller must submit the declaration for that year. The City also says that where no declaration was received, the new owner will be responsible for any charges from the previous owner, so expect the buyer's lawyer to ask about it.

## Your selling checklist

1. Interview at least three agents and check each on RECO's Public Register.
2. Get a price opinion from comparable sales and estimate what you will keep after costs.
3. List any known problems with the house and ask your lawyer which must be disclosed.
4. Ask your lender for the prepayment penalty and about porting.
5. Have your lawyer check title for an expired NOSI or anything else registered.
6. Decide, in writing, how much your agent may share about competing offers.
7. Before closing, arrange the final meter read, cancel pre-authorized payments in writing and note who declares the Vacant Home Tax for the year.
8. Ask your accountant whether the sale needs to be reported on your return.
