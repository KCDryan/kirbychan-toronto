## The short answer

An open building permit in Toronto is a permit that Toronto Building issued for work on a property and never closed. The City of Toronto says a permit stays open until all required inspections are completed and the permit is officially closed. Many stay open for years because nobody booked the final inspection after the deck, the addition or the basement was finished.

For a seller, that old file matters. The City's own page on closing permits says open permits can cause delays when selling a home, because buyers and lenders may want proof that the work was completed and inspected.

This post explains how to find an open permit on a Toronto house, the steps the City sets out to close one and the records worth having ready before you list. It is general information, not legal advice. Ask your real estate lawyer how an open permit affects your own sale.

## Why an open permit matters when you sell a Toronto house

The City of Toronto's Closing Building Permits page lists what an open permit can lead to. Three points matter most to a seller.

- **The sale itself.** The City says open permits may delay or complicate real estate transactions and that in some cases a lawyer may advise against purchasing a property with an outstanding permit.
- **Enforcement.** If uninspected work does not meet safety requirements, Toronto Building may issue orders to comply that require corrective action.
- **New permits.** If an owner applies for a new permit while an older one is still open, Toronto Building may require the old one to be resolved first. A buyer planning a renovation will care about that.

The City is also clear about who carries the file. It says that as the current registered owner of the property, you are responsible for any outstanding building permits, including a permit taken out by a previous homeowner. A buyer who takes title to your house would be in that same position, which is why a buyer's lawyer looks for open permits before closing.

## How to check for an open building permit in Toronto

You have three City sources. They differ in what they show, what they cost and how long they take.

| What to use | What it shows | Cost and timing the City gives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Building Permit Application and Inspection Status tool | Active permits 10 years old or less from the date of application, plus permits closed or cancelled within the last month. Updated as of the previous business day | A free online search |
| Inspection Status Report | Which mandatory inspection stages for one permit have passed, not passed or are outstanding. It does not include inspection details | Bought online with the permit number, an email address and a credit card |
| Property Information Report | Issued building permits on file for the address, the status of inspections and any outstanding orders under the Ontario Building Code, the Zoning By-law or the Property Standards By-law | $214.79 per request effective January 1, 2026. Approximately five business days |

Start with the free search. If your house has had permit work in the last ten years, the status tool should list the permit and its current status. The City's glossary explains each status. The ones a seller is likely to see are below.

| Status | What the City's glossary says |
| --- | --- |
| Permit Issued | The permit has been issued |
| Inspection | The permit has been issued and is under active inspection |
| Work Not Started | An inspection was conducted and the construction has not taken place |
| Violation | There is an Inspection Order against this permit |
| Permit Revoked | The permit has been revoked |

The Property Information Report is the fuller record. The City says it is sometimes called a property compliance letter and that it is typically requested because of a possible sale or refinancing. To apply, email the application form to bldapplications@toronto.ca with "Property Information Report or Compliance letters" in the subject line. The City notes the report does not outline permitted use, so it will not tell you whether a second suite or a home business is allowed.

## How to close an open building permit before you list

The City's steps depend on what happened to the work. Find your situation in the table.

| Your situation | What the City says to do |
| --- | --- |
| The work was finished recently | Schedule a final inspection to verify the work meets the Ontario Building Code |
| The work was started but not finished | Schedule an inspection to assess progress. Toronto Building will provide next steps if more work is required |
| The work never started | Ask Toronto Building to revoke the permit |
| The work was finished but never inspected | Schedule a final inspection. You may need to submit reports from an architect or an engineer confirming compliance |
| A previous owner took out the permit | Confirm with Toronto Building whether any work was done under it. If work was completed but not inspected, request a final inspection and submit any required reports |

### Booking the inspection

The City gives two ways to book.

1. Online through Toronto Building's Inspection Requests portal, which is open at all hours. You need the permit number and the contact details of the person who will meet the inspector on site.
2. By phone. The Closing Building Permits page gives 416-397-5330, Monday to Friday, 8:30 a.m. to 4:30 p.m.

Inspections are carried out Monday to Friday from 8:30 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. The inspector checks whether the construction complies with the approved permit and the Ontario Building Code. If deficiencies are found, you get instructions on what has to be corrected before the permit can be closed.

Once all required inspections are successfully completed, Toronto Building closes the permit and you receive confirmation. Keep that confirmation with your sale documents.

### If you no longer have the drawings

An inspector compares the work with the permit drawings. If you cannot find your copy, the City says you can request the drawings through Toronto Building's records disclosure service. The Request Building Records page says to email the application form to bldrecords@toronto.ca, that Toronto Building aims to process requests within 30 business days and that the fee effective January 1, 2026 is $76.98 per disclosure. ## What a Notice of Intention to Revoke means

Toronto Building sends a Notice of Intention to Revoke when a building permit is more than 18 months old and no inspections have been requested. The City says the notice does not mean the permit has been revoked. It means the permit will be revoked on the date in the letter if you do not respond.

The notice gives you three choices: ask for the permit to be revoked, request an inspection to close it or request an inspection to keep it open and continue the work. To keep it open, the City says you must request an inspection before the deadline and show that construction or demolition has genuinely started.

Revocation suits a permit for work that was never done. It does not tidy up work that was built. The City warns that any work completed under a revoked permit may be considered a violation of the Building Code Act and the Toronto Municipal Code. It also says that starting or resuming the work later means applying for a new permit and paying the application fees again.

If you inherited a house and a notice arrives addressed to a parent, the same rule applies: the current registered owner is responsible. Contact Toronto Building Inspections at 416-397-5330 or TOBldgInsp@toronto.ca to confirm what the permit covered. ## An open permit is not the same as work with no permit

Sellers often mix up the two.

- **An open permit** means the City approved the plans and the paperwork was never finished. The fix is usually an inspection.
- **Work without a permit** means the City never reviewed the work at all.

For the second case, the City's Working Without a Permit page lists the consequences for an owner who finds out a permit was needed after work started. They include an administrative fee equal to 50 per cent of the permit fees for the project, a stop to all work while the application is processed and possibly having to undo the work. The page adds that the homeowner is responsible for those fees or penalties even if a contractor said no permit was needed.

## How long to allow before your Toronto sale

The City publishes some timelines and not others. Plan around the ones it gives.

| Step | Timing the City gives |
| --- | --- |
| Permit status search | Same day. Data is current to the previous business day |
| Property Information Report | Approximately five business days |
| Building records request for drawings | Toronto Building aims for 30 business days |
| Final inspection and any corrections | No published timeline. It depends on inspector availability and what the inspector finds |

Thirty business days is about six weeks. If an inspection then turns up work to correct, a contractor has to fit that in as well. For that reason we suggest sellers run the status search when they first think about selling, not the week the sign goes up.

Here is an illustration. It is not a real property. An owner in [Leaside](/leaside-toronto/) built a rear addition under a permit several years ago and never booked the final inspection. The status tool shows the permit as issued. The owner requests the drawings, books the final inspection, corrects one item the inspector notes and receives confirmation that the permit is closed. Only then does the house go on the market, with the confirmation in the document folder. The same sequence applies to a third-floor addition in [Riverdale](/riverdale-toronto/) or a new deck anywhere in the city.

## A checklist before you list a Toronto house

1. Search your address on the City's permit status tool and note every permit and its status.
2. List the work done on the house in your time, such as decks, additions, basement finishing and structural changes. Match each job to a permit.
3. For any permit that is not closed, book the inspection or ask Toronto Building about revocation if the work never started.
4. If the drawings are missing, send the building records request early.
5. Consider a Property Information Report so you see what a buyer's lawyer may see.
6. Keep the City's closing confirmation and give a copy to your lawyer.
7. Tell your agent about any permit that will still be open when you list, so it can be dealt with properly and not found late.
