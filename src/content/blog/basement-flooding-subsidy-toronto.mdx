## The short answer

The Toronto basement flooding subsidy is the City's Basement Flooding Protection Subsidy Program. As of May 1, 2026 the City expanded it and now offers up to $6,650 per property for flood protection work completed on or after November 12, 2025. It covers a plumbing assessment, backwater valves, a sump pump with a battery backup and cutting a home's weeping tile connection off from the City sewer.

For a family moving up from a condo to a house, this is new ground. In a house, the sump pump, the backwater valve and the drainage around the foundation are yours to look after. The City says every home is at risk of basement flooding, even if it has not happened before. It also says the drainage system on private property is the owner's responsibility.

This post sets out what the program pays, who qualifies and what to look at on a Toronto house before you buy it. It explains how the City's program works. It is not advice on any particular house, so confirm the details for your property with a licensed plumber, your home inspector, your insurer and your lawyer.

## What the Toronto basement flooding subsidy pays in 2026

The City pays up to 80 per cent of the invoiced cost of each eligible item, up to a cap. The caps below apply to work completed on or after November 12, 2025.

| Eligible work | City subsidy | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Home plumbing assessment | Up to 80 per cent, maximum $500 | One time |
| Backwater valve, installed or replaced, with alarm | Up to 80 per cent, maximum $1,600 per device | Up to two devices |
| Sump pump, installed or replaced, with alarm | Up to 80 per cent, maximum $2,250 | One device |
| Battery backup for the sump pump | Up to 80 per cent, maximum $300 | Itemized separately on the invoice |
| Severing and capping a weeping tile (foundation drain) sewer connection | Up to 80 per cent, maximum $400 | Includes labour, materials and taxes |

Add the caps together and you reach the City's figure: $500, plus $3,200 for two valves, plus $2,250, $300 and $400, comes to $6,650.

For work finished before November 12, 2025 the older caps apply: $1,250 for one backwater valve, $1,750 for one sump pump and $400 for pipe severance. Applications for the expanded amounts opened on May 1, 2026.

The plumbing assessment is the useful first step for a new owner. A licensed plumber completes the City's Home Plumbing Assessment Form and explains which parts of the plumbing could contribute to basement flooding. The City says you do not have to carry out the plumber's recommendations to receive the assessment subsidy.

## Who qualifies in the City of Toronto

The program has conditions on the owner, the property and the work. The City lists these.

- You must be the registered owner of a single-family, duplex, triplex or fourplex residential property in the City of Toronto.
- The property must not have used up its lifetime maximum for that type of work.
- The downspouts from the eavestroughs must be disconnected from the City sewer, unless the City has granted an exemption.
- All front yard paved areas, including parking pads, must comply with the City's Zoning By-law.
- You must apply within two years of each installation or service (one year for work completed before November 12, 2025).
- The contractor and any subcontractors must hold a valid City of Toronto business licence at the time of the work.
- You must have no outstanding taxes or debts owed to the City when the application is processed.

The City also says the subsidy is paid first come, first served and depends on annual funding approved by City Council. An application does not guarantee a payment.

## Why the house you buy may already have used part of it

This is the point a buyer is most likely to miss. The City says each property has a lifetime maximum subsidy for each type of eligible work. The limit follows the house, not the owner.

If a previous owner already claimed for a sump pump, there may be nothing left for a new sump pump after you move in. If they claimed for one backwater valve under the expanded program, a second may still qualify.

The City will tell you a property's subsidy history if you contact BasementFlooding@toronto.ca or call 416-338-7668. Before you buy, ask the seller which flood protection devices are in the house, when they were installed, whether a permit was taken out and whether a subsidy was paid. Ask for the invoices. Your lawyer can advise on how to deal with any of this in the agreement of purchase and sale.

## Backwater valves, sump pumps and permits

The two main devices do different jobs.

- **A backwater valve** closes the sewer line during heavy rain to stop water from entering the home. The City warns that while the valve is closed you should not use toilets, sinks, dishwashers or washing machines, because the water has nowhere to go. An alarm tells you when it has closed.
- **A sump pump** pumps the water collected by the weeping tiles out of the house. The City says it should discharge onto a permeable surface such as a lawn, garden or gravel, at least two metres from the foundation wall. A battery backup keeps it running when the power goes out in a storm.

The permit rules are not the same for both. The City's building permit page lists installing a backwater valve as work that needs a building permit. The subsidy requires you to request an inspection once the valve is installed and not to cover or enclose it before the inspection is complete. The same City page says a building permit is not required to install a sump pump.

That gives a buyer something concrete to check. If a valve permit was never closed, it can show on the City's permit status search. A Property Information Report from Toronto Building lists the permits on file for the address. ## What to check on a Toronto house before you buy

Most of these come straight from the City's own list of what owners are responsible for and how basements flood. Use them as questions for the seller and your home inspector.

1. **Downspouts.** Disconnection from the sewer is mandatory for all property owners in Toronto. Look at where each downspout ends. The City wants water directed away from the foundation and not onto a neighbour's property, a driveway or a sidewalk.
2. **Front yard paving.** A parking pad or paved front yard that does not comply with the Zoning By-law makes the property ineligible for the subsidy. Ask the seller whether any front yard parking was approved.
3. **Lot grading.** The City says the ground should slope away from the foundation. Water pooling against the house is a warning sign.
4. **Signs of past water.** The City suggests checking the corners of the basement. A damp or wet floor there can mean the weeping tiles are not working properly.
5. **Weeping tile connection.** In some houses the weeping tiles drain into the sewer. The City recommends disconnecting them and installing a sump pump or backwater valve. A plumber can tell you how your house is set up.
6. **Existing devices.** Find the backwater valve clean-out and the sump pit, if there are any. Ask how old they are and whether they were maintained.
7. **The street.** The City's Basement Flooding Protection Program upgrades sewers in study areas across Toronto and publishes a map. It also says these upgrades do not guarantee flood prevention and do not cover drainage on private property.

Ask the seller directly whether the basement has ever taken on water and whether an insurance claim was made. Then ask your insurer how flood protection devices and any past claim affect your coverage. Insurers set their own terms, so the answer has to come from them.

## Planning the work after you move up

Here is how the timing can work. This is an illustration, not a real property. A family buys a detached house in [Leaside](/leaside-toronto/) that has no backwater valve and an old sump pump. After closing they become the registered owners and can apply.

1. Hire a plumber with a City of Toronto licence for the assessment. The City's table shows three licence types that can do all four kinds of eligible work: Plumbing Contractor (T94), Plumbing and Heating Contractor (T92) and Drain Contractor (T87). A Building Renovator (T85) can do the sump pump and the pipe severance only. Check the licence on the City's Business Licence Lookup or at 416-392-6700 before work starts, because an unlicensed contractor means the City denies the subsidy.
2. Get at least two quotes and references, as the City recommends.
3. Take out the building permit for the backwater valve and book the inspection before the floor is closed up.
4. Ask the contractor to itemize the sump pump and the battery backup separately and mark the invoice "paid in full".
5. Photograph the sump pump and its discharge pipe outside the house.
6. Apply online with the original electronic invoices. Allow up to 10 weeks for payment.

The work and the subsidy belong in the same budget as the rest of the move. ## If water does get into a Toronto basement

The City's Basement Flooding page lists the first steps.

- Call Toronto Hydro to have the power shut off if water has risen near outlets or the electrical panel.
- If you suspect a sewer back-up, avoid using toilets and sinks until the problem is fixed.
- Report the flooding to 311 so City staff can investigate the source.
- Call your insurance company, take photos of the damage and keep receipts for clean-up.

The City says that where flooding comes from a private blocked drainpipe, leaking foundation walls or poor lot drainage, the owner is responsible for repairs. You can still submit a claim to the City, which passes it to the City's insurance adjusters.

## A short checklist for move up buyers

- Ask the seller for a list of flood protection devices, their permits and invoices.
- Ask the City for the property's subsidy history.
- Have your home inspector look at downspouts, grading and the basement corners.
- Budget a plumbing assessment soon after closing. The City covers up to 80 per cent of it, to a maximum of $500.
- Use licensed contractors, the right permit and itemized invoices so the subsidy is not refused.
