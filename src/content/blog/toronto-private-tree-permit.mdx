## The short answer

A Toronto private tree permit is what the City requires before anyone injures or removes a tree on private land that measures 30 centimetres or more in diameter, measured 1.4 metres above the ground. For a family moving up to a house with a deeper lot and a mature canopy, that rule matters long before any shovel goes in, because the trees on the lot and on the lots next door, set the real limits on where an addition, a new garage, a widened driveway or a garden suite can go.

This guide walks through what the City protects, how the measuring works, what changed on September 1, 2026 and the questions worth asking while you are still deciding whether a house is the right one. It is general information drawn from the City's own pages; it is not legal advice and a certified arborist and your designer are the people who can speak to a specific property.

If you are weighing the bigger move itself, the financing, timing and sell-first or buy-first questions are covered on our [upsizing in Toronto](/upsizing-toronto/) page.

## Which trees the City protects

The City's tree and ravine pages describe three groups of protected trees and knowing which group a tree falls into tells you which rules apply.

- **Private trees of 30 centimetres or more.** Any activity that can injure or remove one of these on private property is prohibited unless a permit authorizes it, under the Private Tree Protection Bylaw.
- **Trees on City streets, any size.** The boulevard tree in front of the house belongs to the City and harming or removing it needs a permit no matter how small it is.
- **Trees in ravine and natural feature areas.** Lots that back onto ravines can fall under the separate Ravine and Natural Feature Protection Bylaw, which has its own permits and, since September 1, 2026, its own application fees.

The word "injure" carries real weight here. Digging footings, trenching for services or paving a driveway inside a tree's root area can count as injury even when nobody touches the trunk, which is why a protected tree can shape a renovation plan without ever being cut.

## How to measure a tree the way the City does

The City uses diameter at breast height. Its instructions are simple enough to do on a showing with a tape measure:

1. Measure 1.4 metres (4.5 feet) up from the base of the tree at ground level.
2. Wrap the tape around the trunk at that height, at a right angle to the trunk.
3. Divide the circumference by 3.14. That is the diameter at breast height.

If the result is 30 centimetres or more, the tree is protected. For a tree with several stems, the City uses the largest stem and if one stem reaches 30 centimetres the whole tree, every stem included, is protected. As a rough guide, a trunk with a circumference of about 94 centimetres, a little over three feet, is right at the 30 centimetre line.

## Tree protection zones and why they matter for an addition

The City publishes minimum tree protection zone sizes that grow with the trunk. For a private tree of 30 to 40 centimetres, the minimum zone is 2.4 metres around the tree and the table keeps rising for larger trees, reaching six centimetres of zone for every centimetre of diameter at the top of the scale. For trees in ravines and natural areas the distances are larger again or the dripline, whichever is greater.

For a move-up buyer, that zone is the practical answer to "could we put a family room on the back?" A large maple or oak near the rear of the house can mean the addition has to be narrower, move to another side or use foundations designed around the roots. None of that is a reason to walk away from a house you love; it is a reason to understand the lot before you price in a plan.

## What changed on September 1, 2026

The City's Tree Bylaw Review page lists changes that took effect on September 1, 2026. The ones a buyer is most likely to meet:

- **A Distinctive Tree category** for large, healthy trees on private property and in ravines that are more than 61 centimetres in diameter, giving them added protection.
- **Protection for newly planted trees** that were required through enforcement or development approvals, regardless of their size.
- **Stump diameter as evidence.** A stump of 40 centimetres or more can be used as a secondary measurement when a removed tree's trunk cannot be measured.
- **Diameter-based replacement ratios** for contraventions, starting at five replacement trees for one removed and increasing for larger trees.
- **New application fees** for ravine and natural feature permits, with the City noting that permit fees are updated every year.

The page also says updated standards for recommending fines are expected in the first quarter of 2027, so expect more change rather than less.

## Trees on the line and next door

Many Toronto lots are narrow and plenty of the best trees sit right on a fence line. The City calls a tree whose trunk grows across a property line a boundary tree and treats it as owned by both owners. Either owner can apply for a permit, but the other owner's consent is required and if you disagree with the City about ownership you may need an up-to-date survey.

Neighbouring trees count too. The City's application instructions ask that the site plan show every tree of 30 centimetres or more on the property and on adjacent properties within six metres. A plan for the house you are buying can be shaped by a tree you will never own.

## What a permit application asks for

According to the City's How to Apply page, an application to remove a tree includes:

| Item | What the City asks for |
| --- | --- |
| Application fee | Listed on the current form; fees are non-refundable and updated annually |
| Arborist report | Location, species, size, condition, health and the proposed work and protection measures |
| Landscape and replanting plan | Replacement tree location, species, size and planting season |
| Photos | Clear colour photos of the whole tree and any areas of concern |
| Site plan (construction) | Scaled drawings with existing and proposed structures and all protected trees on the lot and within six metres |

An application to injure a tree follows much the same list and adds a Tree Protection Plan prepared by or with an arborist. The City says incomplete applications are not processed and it encourages applicants to call 311 or their local Tree Protection and Plan Review district office with questions.

If a garden suite is part of your thinking, the City's Garden Suites page says a building permit is required and that tree protection is reviewed as part of the process. ## Questions to ask before you buy

When a house with mature trees is the one, these questions keep the plan grounded in what the lot will really allow:

- **Which trees on the lot, the boulevard and the neighbouring lots within six metres measure 30 centimetres or more?** A tape measure on the showing gives you a first answer.
- **Is any tree on a property line?** If so, any future permit involves the neighbour.
- **Is the lot in a ravine-protected area?** The City's ravine bylaw map answers this and it changes the rules.
- **Did the seller remove or cut back trees recently?** A fresh stump or new planting is worth asking about, since stump size can now be used as evidence.
- **Was the addition or garage you are admiring built with a permit?** Our post on [open building permits in Toronto](/blog/open-building-permit-toronto-selling-house/) explains how to check permit history.

Bring an arborist and a designer in early, before you settle on a price that assumes a particular renovation and ask your lawyer how any open issue should be handled in the agreement.
