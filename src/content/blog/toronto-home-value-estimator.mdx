## The short answer

A Toronto home value estimator is the box on a website where you type an address and get an instant figure for what the home is worth. That number comes from a computer model working with the data it can reach, such as past sale prices and records about the property, without anyone walking through the house. It is a fair place to start when you are curious and a poor place to finish when you are about to list, refinance or settle an estate.

Three other figures get mixed up with the online estimate. Your MPAC assessment is a value for property tax, fixed to January 1, 2016. A comparative market analysis (CMA) is an agent's opinion of value from recent nearby sales. An appraisal is a formal report from a qualified appraiser. This post explains what each one is for, what the online figure cannot see and how to get to a number you can act on. It is general information, not legal, tax or mortgage advice.

## Four Toronto home values compared

Before looking at the estimator itself, it helps to see the four figures side by side. Each one answers a different question.

| Figure | Who produces it | What it is based on | Date it reflects | Best used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Online estimate | A website's computer model | Data the model can reach, with no visit to the house | Whenever the model last updated | A first look out of curiosity |
| MPAC assessment | Municipal Property Assessment Corporation | Sales of similar properties in your area and five key property factors | January 1, 2016 | Calculating Toronto property tax |
| Comparative market analysis | A registered real estate agent | Recent sales of similar homes nearby, adjusted for your home's features | The present market | Setting a list price and planning a sale |
| Appraisal | A qualified appraiser | An inspection plus analysis of comparable properties | The date the assignment sets | Lending, court, estate or tax needs |

The rest of this post works through each row, starting with the one most owners see first.

## How a Toronto home value estimator works

An online estimator is an automated valuation. The site feeds what it knows about an address into a model and the model returns a figure, sometimes with a range. Each site sets its own method and its own data sources, which is why two sites can show quite different numbers for the same house on the same day.

The strength of the approach is speed and scale. A model can produce a figure for every address in the city in seconds and it never gets tired of checking. The weakness is that it only knows what is in its data. If a fact about your house was never recorded or has changed since it was recorded, the model works from the old version.

TRREB's MLS Home Price Index shows how much a model needs to know to be useful. TRREB says the index is calculated with a statistical model that takes into account a home's quantitative features (for example the number of rooms) and its qualitative features (for example whether it has a finished basement). Even with those inputs, TRREB presents the index as a tool to gauge a neighbourhood's home price levels and trends. It describes an area, not the value of your particular house.

## What online figures miss about a Toronto house

The things that move a Toronto sale price the most are often the things a model cannot see. Here are the gaps we come across most often.

- **Condition and renovations.** A house with a new kitchen, updated wiring and a dry, finished basement and a house with original systems can carry the same record. An estimator has no way to tell them apart unless the changes were recorded.
- **Basement space.** MPAC says its exterior square footage does not include the basement, deck, porch or garage. Many Toronto houses have significant living space below grade, which a figure built on recorded square footage may leave out.
- **Layout and light.** Two houses with the same square footage can feel very different inside. Buyers pay for layout, ceiling height and light and none of those show up in a database.
- **The street and the lot.** A model may place two homes in the same area. A buyer standing on the sidewalk notices traffic, a park across the road, a ravine at the back or a shared driveway.
- **The mix of homes nearby.** Toronto streets often hold very different houses side by side: an original bungalow next to a new two-storey rebuild, for example. A model that leans on nearby sales can pull your figure toward homes that are nothing like yours.
- **What buyers are doing this month.** The figure is only as current as the last sales the model has absorbed. Our [Toronto market reports](/market-reports/) show how activity changes from month to month.

The same issue shows up within a single neighbourhood. Our [Leaside guide](/leaside-toronto/) describes an area of mostly detached houses with semi-detached houses and condo apartments as well. Any neighbourhood figure blends those types, which is why our post on the [average home price in Toronto vs the median](/blog/average-home-price-toronto-vs-median/) explains that neither number is the value of a particular home.

## Your MPAC assessment is not market value

Owners often reach for their Property Assessment Notice as a second opinion on the online figure. It answers a different question.

MPAC says it assesses and classifies all properties in Ontario. For residential property, MPAC says it analyzes the sales of similar properties that have sold in your area and names five key factors that affect value: the age of the building (adjusted for major renovations or additions), exterior square footage, location, lot dimensions and quality of construction.

The key difference is the date. MPAC says the fixed valuation date remains January 1, 2016 and that the assessment is what the property would have sold for on that date, reflecting its condition including major changes made since then. MPAC also notes that the Ontario government postponed the 2020 Assessment Update because of the COVID-19 pandemic. The City of Toronto describes the same figure as the current value assessment, what a property could reasonably have sold for on January 1, 2016.

The City uses MPAC's assessed value and classification to calculate your property taxes. If you disagree with the assessment, the City says to file a Request for Reconsideration with MPAC by the deadline printed on your Property Assessment Notice. Our post on [Toronto property tax](/blog/toronto-property-tax/) shows how the assessment turns into your tax bill.

So the assessment and the online estimate are not competing answers. One is a tax value pinned to a past date. The other is a model's view of the market today. Neither is built to set a list price.

## Comparative market analysis vs appraisal

When the decision is real, the two figures that matter are a comparative market analysis from an agent and an appraisal from an appraiser.

### What a comparative market analysis gives you

A CMA is an agent's opinion of what your home would sell for now, built from recent sales of similar homes nearby. The difference from an online estimate is judgement. An agent can see the house, ask what has been done to it and choose the sales that genuinely compare, then adjust for the differences in size, condition, lot and features.

A good CMA shows its working. You should see the sales the agent chose, how your home compares with each one and the range that results. That is how our [free home valuation](/home-valuation/) is prepared: the team reads recent sales of comparable homes near yours and sends the range with the reasoning behind it. It is an opinion for planning a sale, not a formal appraisal.

Choosing who prepares the CMA matters as much as the figure. The Real Estate Council of Ontario (RECO) keeps a Public Register that shows whether a salesperson is registered in Ontario and in good standing. RECO also suggests asking an agent about their trading activity, so you understand the types of homes they have bought or sold recently.

### What an appraisal gives you

An appraisal is a formal written opinion of market value from a qualified appraiser after an inspection. It is the figure a lender, a court or an estate may need. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada says a lender may determine a home's value through a home appraisal of its market value or by using the purchase price. An online estimate is not one of the methods it describes.

Our post on the [Toronto home appraisal](/blog/toronto-home-appraisal/) covers what the appraiser inspects, who the report belongs to and how it differs from your MPAC assessment.

| Question | Comparative market analysis | Appraisal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who prepares it | A registered real estate agent | A qualified appraiser |
| Main purpose | Planning a sale and setting a list price | Lending, court, estate or tax needs |
| Shows the comparable sales | It should, so ask to see them | Yes, in the written report |

## When a Toronto home value estimator is good enough

An online figure has its place. It is a reasonable tool when you are early in your thinking and the stakes are low.

- You are wondering in general terms whether a move is realistic.
- You want a loose sense of how values in your area have moved.
- You are comparing a few streets before you look at any homes seriously.

It is not enough when money or a legal decision depends on the number:

- Setting a list price. Pricing from a model that has not seen the house can leave money behind or keep a home sitting on the market.
- Planning a downsize where the sale funds the next home. Our [downsizing service](/services/downsizing/) explains how we plan the sale alongside the move.
- Settling an estate, where trustees may need a figure they can explain to the beneficiaries. Our [estate sales service](/services/estate-sales/) explains how we work to the estate lawyer's timeline.
- Refinancing or borrowing against your home, where the lender decides how value is determined.

## How to get a number you can act on

If you are thinking about selling a Toronto home, these steps turn a rough online figure into a working plan.

1. **Use the online estimate as a starting point only.** Note the figure and the range, then put it to one side.
2. **Find your MPAC assessment and read it for what it is.** It tells you your tax value as of January 1, 2016, not today's price.
3. **List what a model cannot know.** Write down renovations, the finished basement, new systems, the lot and anything unusual about the street.
4. **Ask for a comparative market analysis that shows its comparable sales.** Check the agent on RECO's Public Register and ask about the homes they have recently sold.
5. **Hire an appraiser if a lender, court or estate needs a formal report.** Your lender or lawyer will tell you whether one is required.
6. **Read the full sequence of a sale.** Our guide on how to [sell a home in Toronto](/blog/sell-home-in-toronto/) walks through each step from choosing an agent to closing and our [sellers' page](/sellers/) explains how we run a sale.

For anything that turns on your own tax, legal or mortgage position, speak with an accountant, a lawyer or a mortgage professional before you rely on any figure.
