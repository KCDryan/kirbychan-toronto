## The short answer

When a parent is elderly or unwell, the question "is it better to sell a house before or after death" usually comes up sooner or later. There is no single right answer. The two paths are taxed under the same federal rules, but they differ in who signs, what the court has to do, how long the house sits empty and what Ontario's Estate Administration Tax is charged on.

The principal residence exemption from the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) can apply on either path. Selling first does not automatically avoid probate or its tax. Selling after death means the estate trustee usually needs a court certificate before signing a deal, so the house may be carried for months.

This post compares the two paths side by side. It is general information and not legal or tax advice. Every family's will, ownership and health situation is different, so take the comparison to an estates lawyer and an accountant before deciding.

## Before or after death: the two paths compared

| Question | Selling during the owner's life | Selling after death as an estate home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who signs | The owner or their attorney under a continuing power of attorney for property | The estate trustee |
| Court step for the house | None for the sale itself | An estate certificate should be obtained before an Agreement of Purchase and Sale |
| Tax on the gain | Reported on the owner's return, Schedule 3 and Form T2091(IND) | Deemed sale at fair market value just before death, reported on the final return with Form T1255 |
| Estate Administration Tax | Charged on whatever money is left in the estate if a certificate is issued | Charged on the house's appraised value at death, plus other assets, if a certificate is issued |
| Empty house costs | Only if the owner has moved out, for example into care | Usually from the date of death until closing |
| Who decides the price and timing | The owner or their attorney | The estate trustee, subject to the will and the Estates Administration Act |

The rest of this post goes through each row. Our guide to [selling an estate home in Toronto](/selling-an-estate-home-toronto/) covers the full estate process from start to finish.

## How the tax on the gain works on each path

### Selling during life

The CRA says that if a property was solely your principal residence for every year you owned it, you do not have to pay tax on the gain. The owner still has to report the sale. The CRA says it will only allow the exemption if the sale and the designation are reported on the return for that year, using Schedule 3 (Capital Gains or Losses) and Form T2091(IND).

If an attorney sells the house for a parent who can no longer manage their affairs, the sale is still the parent's sale and goes on the parent's return.

### Selling after death

The CRA says that when a person dies, they are considered to have sold all their property just prior to death, even though there is no actual sale. This is called a deemed disposition. The house is treated as sold at its fair market value immediately before death and any gain or loss is reported on Schedule 3 of the final return.

If the house was a principal residence, the exemption can cover some or all of that gain. The CRA says that even where the entire gain is exempt, the legal representative must still designate the property on the final return by completing Form T1255. The designation only applies until the date of death.

Two exceptions change the picture:

- **A surviving spouse.** The CRA says property transferred to a surviving spouse or common-law partner who is a resident of Canada may not result in a capital gain or loss on the final return. The gain is postponed until the spouse sells.
- **A beneficiary who moves in.** After death, the CRA says the beneficiary may be able to designate the property as their own principal residence if they meet the required conditions.

The final return is due April 30 of the year after the death if the person died between January 1 and October 31. For a death between November 1 and December 31, it is due six months after the date of death. Our post on [selling a parent's house after death and the Ontario taxes](/blog/selling-parents-house-after-death-ontario-taxes/) covers the final return, the estate's own return and any change in value between death and the sale.

## Does selling first avoid probate and the estate tax?

This is the part that is easiest to misread. Selling the house during life turns it into money. It does not take that money out of the estate.

Ontario charges Estate Administration Tax on the value of the estate if an estate certificate is applied for and issued. The value includes all assets the person owned at the time of death, including bank accounts and investments as well as real estate. If no certificate is applied for or issued, no tax is owed. Ontario also says that if the deceased owned real property or assets held by a financial institution, the estate normally must be probated.

For applications made on or after January 1, 2020, Ontario sets the tax this way:

- No tax if the estate is worth $50,000 or less.
- Above that, $15 for every $1,000 (or part of $1,000) of the value, with the value rounded up to the nearest thousand.
- A mortgage or lien on the real estate can be deducted. Real estate commissions, legal fees and funeral expenses cannot.

Here is an illustration with round numbers, not a real family.

| Step | House sold after death | House sold during life |
| --- | --- | --- |
| House at its appraised value at death | $1,200,000 | Not owned at death |
| Money in the bank at death | $100,000 | $1,150,000 (the sale money left after costs, for illustration) |
| Estate value | $1,300,000 | $1,150,000 |
| Less the first $50,000 | $1,250,000 | $1,100,000 |
| Tax at $15 for each $1,000 | $18,750 | $16,500 |

The tax follows the value left in the estate, not the type of asset. In the second column it is lower only because less money was left, not because the house was sold. Ontario also says to use the appraised value at the date of death even if the house sells for more or less shortly after, which is why estates need a supportable value. Our [free home valuation](/home-valuation/) can be a starting point for that conversation with your lawyer.

Which assets pass outside the estate, such as property held jointly with a right of survivorship, is a planning question for an estates lawyer. Ontario's page lists jointly owned assets that automatically become the other owner's among the assets not included in the estate's value.

## Who can sign: the owner, an attorney or the estate trustee

While the owner is alive and capable, they make the decision. If they cannot, Ontario says an attorney for property can make decisions about their financial affairs, including maintaining or selling the house. Our post on [selling a parent's house with power of attorney in Ontario](/blog/selling-a-parents-house-with-power-of-attorney-ontario/) explains what the attorney needs to show the buyer's lawyer.

That authority has a hard stop. Section 12 of the Substitute Decisions Act says a continuing power of attorney is terminated when the grantor dies. If a parent dies after a sale is firm but before it closes, the attorney can no longer sign and the estate trustee takes over. Ask your lawyer at the start what would happen to a firm deal in that case.

After death, the estate trustee sells. Ontario's probate page says that where the deceased's real property must be sold, an estate certificate should be obtained before anyone enters into an Agreement of Purchase and Sale. Ontario says applications are typically processed within 15 business days, but it may take longer if documents are missing or a judge has to decide an issue. Our post on [whether you can sell a house before probate in Ontario](/blog/selling-a-house-before-probate-ontario/) covers that step in detail.

The Estates Administration Act adds two rules for the estate trustee:

1. **Beneficiaries and the sale.** Section 17 says beneficiaries do not have to agree to a sale made to pay debts. A sale made only to distribute the estate needs the approval of a majority of the beneficiaries representing at least half of the interests, with special rules for minors and people who are mentally incapable.
2. **Three years.** Section 9 says real property not sold or distributed within three years after the death vests in the beneficiaries, unless the estate trustee has registered a caution in the land registry office.

## Carrying an empty house in Toronto

Whichever path you take, an empty house keeps costing money. Property tax and utilities continue until closing. Our post on [Toronto property tax](/blog/toronto-property-tax/) explains how the bill is set and adjusted on a sale.

Toronto adds the Vacant Home Tax. The City requires residential owners to declare the occupancy status of their property every year, even if they qualify for an exemption. If no declaration is received by the April 30 deadline, the City assumes the property was vacant and sends a bill. Beginning with the 2024 taxation year, the rate is 3% of the property's Current Value Assessment.

The City lists exemptions that fit both paths:

- **Before death, owner in care.** The principal resident of the vacant property is in a hospital, long-term or supportive care facility for at least six months during the year. This can be claimed for up to two consecutive taxation years.
- **After death.** The property was vacant for six months or more in the year due to the death of an owner. This can be claimed for up to three consecutive taxation years and needs a copy of the death certificate.

Both still have to be claimed through the annual declaration, with supporting documents.

## Is it better to sell a house before or after death for your family?

The tax rules above apply in the same way to everyone. What tips the decision is usually practical. These are the questions we suggest families work through with their lawyer and accountant:

1. Is the owner still capable of deciding and is there a continuing power of attorney for property if they are not?
2. Does the owner need the money from the house during their life, for example to pay for care?
3. Was the house solely the owner's principal residence for every year they owned it? Or was there a cottage, a rental unit or a second property?
4. Is there a surviving spouse or a beneficiary who plans to live in the house?
5. Will the estate need a certificate anyway because of bank accounts or other assets?
6. Who would carry the property tax, utilities and upkeep while the house is empty and for how long?
7. Do the beneficiaries agree on selling or keeping the house?
