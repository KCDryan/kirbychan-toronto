## The short answer

A Toronto heritage listed property is one the City of Toronto has added to its Heritage Register because it believes the property may have cultural heritage value, without designating it. For a buyer, listing is a flag to look into rather than a ban on changes. The City says it does not require a heritage permit and does not stop renovation or development. It does give the City time to consider designation if the owner later wants to demolish.

Designated properties and homes inside a heritage conservation district are different and the rules are tighter. This post sets out the three categories, what each means for a house you plan to renovate or rebuild, a provincial deadline on January 1, 2027 that affects many listed homes and how to check an address before you make an offer. It is general information, not legal advice. Your lawyer can confirm what applies to a specific property.

## What the Toronto Heritage Register is

The Heritage Register is the City's public list of properties with cultural heritage value or interest. Under section 27 of the Ontario Heritage Act it includes every designated property and may also include properties that are listed. It is not a single type of protection. It holds three kinds of entry:

| Type | What it means | Rules for the owner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Listed | Believed to have heritage value but not designated. No by-law on title | No heritage permit. At least 60 days' notice to Council before demolishing or removing a structure |
| Designated (Part IV) | Protected individually. A by-law is registered on title. The property must meet two or more criteria under Ontario Regulation 9/06 | Heritage permit before changes |
| In a heritage conservation district (Part V) | Protected as part of an area under a municipal by-law | Heritage permit for new construction, exterior alterations and additions, as the district plan sets out |

The City says that listed means further evaluation will take place if there is an intent to demolish. It also says designation usually follows a demolition notice, a planning application or an owner's request linked to an incentive program.

## What listing does and does not change for a buyer

The City is direct on the limits. Listing does not add maintenance requirements beyond the property standards that already apply. It does not stop renovation or development and it does not let the City withhold a permit for alterations that are not demolition.

What it does add:

- **The 60-day notice.** The owner must give Council at least 60 days' notice of an intention to demolish or remove a structure. Ontario's guide says the Building Code makes this notice a condition for issuing a permit and that the pause gives Council time to discuss conservation or begin designation.
- **Interim protection.** The province describes listing as giving interim protection from demolition or removal while the City considers designation.
- **A heritage study on larger projects.** The City says most planning applications involving heritage properties need a Heritage Impact Assessment, an independent professional study done at the earliest stage of project planning.

For a buyer who plans to renovate a listed house, nothing in the City's description requires a heritage permit. A buyer who plans to tear the house down and rebuild should expect the 60-day notice to be part of the timeline and should talk to a lawyer and a designer before firming up an offer. Our post on [buying a Willowdale house to rebuild](/blog/willowdale-real-estate/) walks through the other City steps for a rebuild.

## Designated and district properties: where a permit is needed

For a designated property the City says owners need a heritage permit before making changes. The City's page on application requirements refers owners to its Heritage Permit Guide for what counts as a change, so read it for the exact work you have in mind.

A heritage conservation district is an area protected by a municipal by-law under Part V of the Ontario Heritage Act. The City says new construction, exterior alterations and additions to properties in a district need a heritage permit. Work that is not visible from the street, interior work and routine maintenance do not. A standalone permit costs nothing to apply for and the City says most are decided within a week. Each district plan explains when a permit is needed for that district.

The City's list of districts runs to 25 entries, including Cabbagetown and Rosedale, each listed in several parts. Our guides to [The Annex](/the-annex-toronto/) and [Rosedale](/rosedale-toronto/) cover two of the areas with districts and our [High Park and The Annex comparison](/blog/high-park-toronto-real-estate/) sets out the East Annex and Madison Avenue districts.

## The January 1, 2027 deadline for older listings

Ontario changed the rules for listed properties. For a property added to a municipal register on or after January 1, 2023, Council has two years to issue a Notice of Intention to Designate. If it does not, the property comes off the register.

Older listings get a fixed date. The province calls properties on a register as of December 31, 2022 legacy listed properties. They must be dealt with by January 1, 2027 or be removed. A property removed this way cannot be added back as a listed property for five years, although it can still be designated later.

In practice this means two things for a buyer:

1. A house that is listed today may not be on the Register after January 1, 2027, unless the City has moved to designate it.
2. A house that is not listed today can still be designated. Ontario's guide says that after certain planning events a Notice of Intention to Designate can only be issued if the property was already on the register. Council then has 90 days to act.

We have not found a City list of which legacy listings will be designated, so do not assume a listed house will or will not stay on the Register. Check the status again close to your closing date.

## How to check a Toronto address before you make an offer

The City gives four routes:

1. **Heritage Property Search.** Search by address. The City says it is the most up-to-date way of confirming whether a property is on the Register.
2. **Heritage Property Map.** The same information on a map, useful for seeing whether a street sits in a district.
3. **The list of heritage conservation districts.** Each adopted district links to its plan.
4. **Designation by-law lookup.** For a property designated before 1998 the City directs you to City Archives at 416-392-5431. For a property designated after 1998 you use the By-law Status Registry.

Then look at the surrounding area. The City's [Application Information Centre](https://www.toronto.ca/city-government/planning-development/application-information-centre/) lists active planning applications, which can show a development next door that involves a heritage property.

## Questions to ask the seller and your lawyer

- Is the property listed, designated or in a district, according to the City's search tool today?
- If it is designated, has any past work needed a heritage permit and is the paperwork available?
- Has the City issued a Notice of Intention to Designate or has the owner served a notice of objection?
- Does the property's title show a designation by-law?
- Does your plan involve demolition and if so how does the 60-day notice fit your closing and construction dates?

The City's page also lists a Heritage Grant Program and a Heritage Tax Rebate Program. It does not describe their terms on the Register page, so ask the City whether either applies to a specific property before you count on it. ## A checklist before you offer on a Toronto heritage property

1. Search the address in the City's Heritage Property Search tool and save the result with the date.
2. Check whether the street is in a heritage conservation district.
3. If the house is designated, read the designation by-law and the Heritage Permit Guide for the work you plan.
4. If you may demolish, plan for the 60-day notice and ask your lawyer about the timing.
5. Check the status again shortly before closing, because of the January 1, 2027 rule.
6. Ask your lawyer to confirm any heritage matters on title.
