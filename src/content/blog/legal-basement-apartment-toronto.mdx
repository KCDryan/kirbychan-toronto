## The short answer

A legal basement apartment in Toronto is a second suite that was built with a City of Toronto building permit and passed its inspections. When a listing promises one, the words in the description are the seller's claim. The proof is in the permit file at Toronto Building.

For a first time buyer, the suite often matters because of the rent it could bring in. That makes it worth checking before you offer, not after. This post explains what the City counts as a secondary suite, which records to ask for, what Toronto Fire Services says about older two-unit houses and what to look at during a showing.

It is general information, not legal, tax or mortgage advice. Your real estate lawyer, a building professional and your lender should look at your own situation.

## What Toronto counts as a secondary suite

The City of Toronto describes a secondary suite as additional living accommodation located within and subordinate to a dwelling unit in a detached house, semi-detached house or townhouse. Its building permit guide says the second unit must be smaller in area than the primary unit and that a basement apartment is a common example.

Ontario's guide for homeowners defines a second unit as a self-contained dwelling unit with a private kitchen, bathroom facilities and sleeping areas. A basement with a bedroom and a bathroom but no kitchen is a finished basement, not a second unit.

Toronto now also permits larger multiplexes. The City's Multiplex Housing page says four-unit multiplexes are permitted city-wide and that six-unit permissions vary by location. It adds that owners still need building permits and must meet zoning rules. If you are looking at a house with more than one extra unit, the same questions in this post apply to each unit.

## Does a basement apartment need a permit in Toronto?

Yes, if it was added as a second suite. The City's page on when you need a building permit lists finishing a basement as permit work when it includes adding a second suite, constructing a basement entrance, underpinning, structural changes or new heating or plumbing. The same page says a change of use, such as turning a single dwelling unit house into a multi-dwelling unit house, needs a permit even if no construction is proposed.

A basement can be finished without a permit only in narrow cases. The City says that is when the work involves no structural or material alterations, creates no additional dwelling unit and adds no new plumbing. A kitchen and a separate entrance downstairs are therefore a reason to ask questions.

### What the City reviews before it issues a permit

The Secondary Suites guide, modified November 3, 2026, lists what an owner submits. That includes fully dimensioned floor plans showing the use of every space and the location of smoke alarms, carbon monoxide detectors and plumbing fixtures, plus building sections showing ceiling heights and stair details. Applications go through Toronto Building Online Services.

The guide also lists a Rental Renovation Licence Screening Form for permits that involve repairs or renovations of a residential unit, which helps the City decide whether a rental renovation licence under Toronto Municipal Code Chapter 662 is also needed.

## How to check the permit history of a Toronto house

Start with the seller and confirm with the City.

1. Ask the listing agent, in writing, for the building permit number for the suite and proof that the permit was closed after a final inspection.
2. Ask for the electrical permit and inspection record. Ontario's guide says the electrical work in a second unit needs a separate permit and an inspection by the Electrical Safety Authority (ESA).
3. Apply for Toronto Building records. The City's Request Building Records page says to email the application form to bldrecords@toronto.ca, that Toronto Building aims to process requests within 30 business days and that the fee effective January 1, 2026 is $76.98 per disclosure.
4. Expect limits on what you can see. Under the City's disclosure policy, issued plans for wholly residential buildings submitted after December 31, 2006 are open to the public only from the 22nd day after the permit application to three months after the inspector closes the file. Outside that window, plans are released to the owner or someone with the owner's written consent, so you may need the seller's help.
5. Give your lawyer the results before your conditions expire. Thirty business days can be longer than a conditional period, so send the request as early as you can.

## Older Toronto two-unit houses and the Ontario Fire Code

Toronto Fire Services says Section 9.8 of the Ontario Fire Code applies to two-unit houses that already existed on or before July 14, 1994, where one suite is wholly or partly above the other or the two sit side by side and share an interior means of escape.

Toronto Fire Services also lists the conditions it sees in two-unit houses that raise the risk of fire and injury:

- a suite added without City review, with no building or zoning permits
- no dedicated exit leading directly outside at ground level or an exit that passes through the other unit
- no interconnected smoke and carbon monoxide alarms or fire alarm system
- bedrooms rented out separately, which can turn the house into a rooming house
- electrical upgrades that were never inspected and approved by ESA

If the seller says the suite dates from before July 1994, ask what records support that date. For a fire safety question about a specific house, contact Toronto Fire Services.

## What to look at during a showing

You cannot judge Building Code compliance from a walk-through, but you can see whether the basics are there. Ontario's guide for homeowners sets out minimum standards for a second unit in an existing house, which it defines as one more than five years old.

| Item | What Ontario's guide says | What to look for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ceiling height | 1.95 metres over the entire required floor area of a basement unit, including the route to the exit | Measure under beams and ducts as well as the middle of the room |
| Windows | Living and dining rooms need window area of 5% of the floor area, bedrooms 2.5% | Windows in every bedroom and the living area |
| Fire separation | A 30-minute fire separation between the units and between units and common areas | Drywalled ceilings and a self-closing door between the units |
| Smoke alarms | Alarms meeting CAN/ULC S531 with a flashing light, on every level, in each bedroom of the second unit and in shared areas | Alarms in the suite bedrooms and the shared laundry |
| Heating | One furnace may serve both units if a duct-type smoke detector shuts it down | Ask whether that detector was installed |
| Plumbing | Each unit has its own water shut-off valve | Ask where the suite's shut-off is |

These are minimums from the provincial guide. A home inspector or a qualified designer can tell you much more. Only Toronto Building can tell you whether the suite was approved.

## Why the paperwork matters for a first time buyer

A basement suite can change how a first home works financially, which is exactly why it deserves the same scrutiny as the price. Whether a lender counts rent from the suite is the lender's decision. Ask a mortgage professional what evidence they need. Run the numbers with and without the rent on our [Toronto mortgage calculator](/mortgage-calculator-toronto/).

If the suite has no permit, ask Toronto Building what a permit would involve before you count on the rent. For a sense of scale, the City's guide lists 2026 permit fees for interior alterations of $11.53 per square metre plus $56.33 for each new residential unit. Design, construction and any required changes cost more on top of that.

Renting out the suite also makes you a landlord, with Ontario rules on leases and rent increases. Our [investment property guide for Toronto](/investment-property-toronto/) covers those rules. ## Questions to ask before you offer on a Toronto house with a suite

- What is the building permit number for the suite and was the permit closed after final inspection?
- Is there an ESA permit and inspection record for the suite's electrical work?
- When was the suite created and what records show that date?
- Is the suite rented now and what does the tenant's lease say?
- Does the suite have its own exit to the outside at ground level?
- Are the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms interconnected between the units?
