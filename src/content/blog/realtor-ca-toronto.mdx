## The short answer

A REALTOR.ca Toronto search is one way buyers look at homes listed for sale in the city. Sellers use it too, to see their own listing the way buyers will. The portal pulls listings from the MLS® Systems run by real estate boards, so a Toronto home shows up because the seller's brokerage entered it. What you see is the seller's advertising: useful for finding homes, not a final word on zoning, schools, tax or condo rules.

This post explains where the listings come from, what to check on a Toronto listing before you book a showing, what Ontario's rules say once you call the agent named on it and what sellers should know about how their own listing gets there. It is general information written by our team. It is not produced, reviewed or endorsed by REALTOR.ca or the Canadian Real Estate Association (CREA).

## What REALTOR.ca is and who runs it

CREA describes REALTOR.ca as the only real estate portal that combines MLS® System feeds from every real estate board and association in the country. Since January 2025 it has been run by REALTOR.ca Canada Inc., a taxable subsidiary wholly owned by CREA. Members voted for that change at a special general meeting on October 23, 2024. CREA said at the time that consumers would not see any immediate changes to their experience.

Two trademarks are worth understanding, because they appear on every listing:

- **REALTOR®** identifies real estate professionals who are members of CREA. CREA itself says the word is not a synonym for "real estate agent" or "broker".
- **MLS®** refers to the co-operative selling systems that local boards license from CREA. CREA calls it a standard of service rather than a thing.

CREA also says that only a real estate professional who is a REALTOR® can list a property on their board's MLS® System. That is why a private owner cannot put a home on an MLS® System without a REALTOR®.

## What REALTOR.ca Toronto listings show and who writes them

Every listing is prepared by the brokerage acting for the seller. The description, the photos, the room sizes and the figures for property tax or condo fees come from that brokerage and the seller. None of it replaces your own checks and a listing is not a contract. The agreement of purchase and sale and your lawyer's title search are what count.

The asking price is the seller's number. It is not an appraisal or a forecast of what the home will sell for. How to read a price and how condo prices compare by size is covered in our post on [Toronto condo prices per square foot](/blog/toronto-condo-prices-per-square-foot/).

The portal is national. If you are searching across the city line, remember that the City of Toronto's rules on zoning, property tax and the municipal land transfer tax apply only to property inside the City of Toronto.

## What to check on a Toronto listing before you book a showing

A listing gives you a starting point. These are the checks we suggest for any Toronto home you are serious about, with the place to confirm each one.

| What the listing says | Where to confirm it | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zoning, lot size, "room to build" | The City's Zoning By-law Interactive Map | Sets what can be built or added on the lot |
| School names | TDSB or TCDSB lookup by address | Boundaries are set by the board and can change |
| Annual property tax | Your lawyer and our [Toronto property tax guide](/blog/toronto-property-tax/) | The figure in a listing may not be the current bill |
| Condo fees, rules, reserve fund | The status certificate | Our [condo status certificate guide](/blog/condo-status-certificate-toronto/) explains what to read |
| Land transfer tax | Our [land transfer tax calculator](/land-transfer-tax-calculator-toronto/) | City of Toronto buyers pay the Ontario tax plus the municipal tax |
| Distance to the subway | Count the stops on the [map of Toronto](/map-of-toronto/) | Descriptions of transit vary from listing to listing |

### Zoning

The City says to use its Zoning By-law Interactive Map to find the zoning on a property. Zoning By-law 569-2013 is the city-wide by-law, but properties shown in grey on the map are still covered by a former municipal by-law. The City adds that the map is for convenience only and that the certified documents held by the City Clerk are what count for legal and planning purposes. If a listing says a lot is suitable for a garden suite, a laneway suite or an addition, treat it as a question for a planner, not a promise.

### Schools

The TDSB lets you find the designated school by entering a home address and notes that pathways are subject to change based on operational and program needs. Catholic school boundaries are a question for the Toronto Catholic District School Board (TCDSB). Confirm the exact address with the board before you rely on a school named in a listing.

### Commute

Many listings mention transit. Count the stops yourself. Our [subway commute guide](/blog/toronto-subway-commute-by-neighbourhood/) does that for the neighbourhoods we cover.

## Contacting the listing agent: what Ontario's rules say

Calling the agent named on a listing is allowed, but it helps to know whose side they are on. The Real Estate Council of Ontario (RECO) sets out the rules.

- **The listing agent works for the seller.** RECO says the agent has a duty to do what is best for their seller client, even if you are only asking about the property. Anything you tell them can be passed on, so be careful what you share about your budget or your timing.
- **They cannot advise you.** If you are not a client of their brokerage, you are a self-represented party. RECO says the agent cannot give you any services, opinions or advice and cannot encourage you to represent yourself or discourage you from working with another agent.
- **You will get a form first.** Before giving a self-represented party any help, the agent must provide RECO's Information and Disclosure to Self-represented Party form and the RECO Information Guide. Any help they give serves their client.
- **You can change your mind.** RECO says you can choose to become a client of a brokerage at any point during the transaction. It also recommends independent professional advice before you go ahead as a self-represented party.

### If you want an agent to search for you

Since December 1, 2023, RECO says brokerages can provide services only to a client under a representation agreement. Any agreement to provide services, even limited ones, counts as one. RECO suggests interviewing at least three agents, checking each one on RECO's Public Register, asking how they will search for suitable properties and how they will negotiate, then reviewing the agreement carefully before you sign. ## For sellers: how your home gets onto REALTOR.ca

A seller does not post a listing on the portal. Your brokerage enters it on its board's MLS® System. The board's feed carries it from there.

CREA's REALTOR® Co-operation Policy came into effect on January 3, 2024. Under it, once a REALTOR® markets a listing to the public, it must go on an MLS® System within the time set by the board or association, which can be up to three days. CREA says exclusive listings remain an option for sellers who do not want broad public exposure: they can still be marketed within the brokerage's own offices and one to one with other REALTORS®.

Before you sign a listing agreement, RECO suggests asking how the home will be marketed. It gives staging, professional photos, advertising and open houses as services a brokerage may offer. It also notes that commissions and fees vary between brokerages. Our posts on [real estate commission in Ontario](/blog/selling-a-house-in-ontario-commission/) and on [how to sell a home in Toronto step by step](/blog/sell-home-in-toronto/) cover those decisions. Our [sellers page](/sellers/) explains how we run a sale.

Once the listing is live, read it the way a buyer would. Check the room sizes, the description and every photo. If something is wrong, ask your agent to correct it.

### Before you set a price

Looking at other asking prices on the portal tells you what sellers hope to get. It does not tell you what your home is worth. A [home valuation](/home-valuation/) from comparable properties is a better starting point for a list price.

## What to do next

1. Shortlist homes on any search you like.
2. For each one, check the zoning map, the school board lookup and the land transfer tax.
3. For a condo, ask for the status certificate before your offer is firm.
4. Decide whether you want representation before you call a listing agent.
5. If you are selling, read your own listing as a buyer would.
